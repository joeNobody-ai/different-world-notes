Lingo & slang

The vocabulary the two series run on, and the vocabulary the culture around them uses. Every entry carries a status, because this project does not pretend to know what was said on screen. Documented means the term appears in the material this site already verifies — an episode's published summary, or a 2026 episode title — and the citation is given. Era lexicon means the term is genuine vocabulary of that period, sourced to dictionaries and scholarship, but has not been verified as used on screen. Handle with care means racialised or derogatory vocabulary that belongs in analysis and never in speech.

113 entries: 18 documented · 76 era lexicon · 8 handle with care · 11 project jargon. Last updated 2026-10-04.

  • documented Appears in the documented evidence base (episode summary or 2026 title) — citation given
  • era lexicon Real vocabulary of the era, from dictionaries and scholarship — not verified as said on screen
  • handle with care Racialised or derogatory term: analysed, never spoken — see the warning on each
  • project jargon A term this project uses for its own method

How to use this list

  • Slang is evidence, not decoration. What a text calls a thing tells you what the culture around it assumes about that thing.
  • Do not separate the eras by taste. 'Cuffing season' and 'going together' are two answers to the same structural question: how a relationship is named, by whom, and for how long.
  • The deepest layer is the oldest: signifying and the dozens are verbal traditions the 1987-1993 series actually put on screen, and the 2026 season inherits both.
  • Never use the handle-with-care vocabulary in your own voice, and never ask anyone to explain or perform it. It is the object of study, not the instrument.

Verbal traditions

The oldest layer, and the one worth teaching hardest: insult, praise and social control carried in the same mode. One of these was an episode's explicit subject.

signifying / signifyin' era lexicon
Indirect, artful verbal critique — insults, boasts and commentary delivered through implication, so the target must decode them.
  1. the speech practice — Indirect, artful verbal critique — boasting, insult, commentary and warning carried by implication.
  2. the literary theory sense — Signifyin(g) as a theory of Black literary practice: repetition with a difference, revision as creation. The spelling with the bracketed (g) marks this second life.

reading (someone) era lexicon
Naming a person's faults publicly and precisely. The criterion is accuracy: a bad read fails, a good one is quoted for years.
  1. the insult sense — To name a person's faults in public, with accuracy as the criterion — a bad read fails and is forgotten.
  2. the skill sense — 'She can read' — an acknowledged competence, judged by wit and precision, not by kindness.
  3. the ordinary sense — Reading text. The course lives on this ambiguity: a text that 'reads' its characters is doing the first thing, on purpose.

throwing shade era lexicon
Indirect disparagement, often by implication rather than statement. A ball-culture term that went mainstream after the 1990s.
  1. literal — Shadow — the OED sense, still the primary one outside the idiom.
  2. the idiom — Indirect disparagement: the target must work out that they were insulted. Distinguished from a direct insult precisely by the deniability.
  3. as a verb — 'To shade someone' — to deliver it. Grammatically transitive, which is what makes it a practice rather than just a noun.

call and response era lexicon
Audience completion of a speaker's line, shared by Black church practice, bands, comedy and classrooms.
  1. the institution — Church — in Black American life a central institution of community, politics and music, and the origin of much of the register both series rely on.
  2. the affirmation — 'Church!' as agreement: the congregation's role crossing from the sanctuary into general speech.
  3. the classroom — A student completing the professor's line, or a room answering a performer. It is the same move in every setting, and it is why a Black classroom can sound like a call and a response at once.

2026 register (contemporary)

cuff / cuffing season documented
The cold-months scramble to pair up, named as a season with a start and an end. The 2026 season builds Episode 4 around it — and the title rewrites the original's 'those who can't teach, tutor' as 'those who can't commit, cuff'.
  1. the season — The cold months, when pairing up is treated as a timed collective project.
  2. the act — To cuff someone: to secure a partner before the window closes. Implies acquisition, with a hint of reluctance.
  3. the joke the title makes — The 2026 episode title rewrites the original's 'those who can't teach, tutor' as 'those who can't commit, cuff' — the same sentence shape, a different failure.

This project's own jargon

Terms the sites and the course use for their own method. A reader meeting them for the first time should not have to infer them.

callback project jargon
A 2026 element that returns to something in the 1987–1993 series — a title, a character, an institution, a storyline.
title echo project jargon
A callback carried in the episode's name, where the rewrite is the joke: 'Reconcilable Differences' becomes 'Irreconcilable Differences'.
tier project jargon
The kind of callback: lore (an institution or place), theme (an idea or storyline), cast (a returning actor or character), or title echo.

How well evidenced a claim is. 'Verified' means traceable to a published record; 'interpretation' means it is a reading that could be argued with.

Documented (a checkable fact), interpretation (a reading of what a text argues) and hypothesis (a claim put up for testing). The course grades on keeping them apart.

Method, and what this page will not claim

This project has not watched either series. So the honest split is: a term appears in a documented summary or a 2026 episode title, or it does not. The first kind is marked documented with its citation. Everything else is era lexicon — real vocabulary of the period, taken from dictionaries and scholarship on African American English, and explicitly not asserted as dialogue. When the transcript pipeline in this project is fed real subtitles, entries can be promoted from era lexicon to documented, one line at a time, with the line recorded.

Definitions of contemporary vocabulary move fast and are contested. Where a term's meaning is still settling, the gloss says what the term does in use rather than pretending to a settled definition.
